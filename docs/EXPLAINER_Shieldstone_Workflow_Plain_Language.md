# Shieldstone Underwriting Workflow - Plain Language Explanation

**For Complete Beginners**

This document explains the Shieldstone master workflow in simple, non-technical terms. Think of it like a step-by-step guide to evaluating whether you should buy an apartment building.

---

## What is Underwriting? (The Big Picture)

**Underwriting** is the process of carefully examining a real estate deal to decide if it's a good investment. Think of it like a home inspection, but instead of checking if the roof leaks, you're checking if the deal makes financial sense.

When someone wants to buy an apartment building (called a "multifamily property"), they need to answer questions like:
- Is this property a good deal?
- Will I make money on this investment?
- What are the risks?
- Should I proceed with buying it, or walk away?

The Shieldstone workflow is a systematic 8-step process that helps answer these questions in a logical, defensible way.

---

## The Core Philosophy

Before diving into the steps, it's important to understand the **philosophy** behind this system:

1. **Economics determine viability, not arbitrary rules** - Just because a building is old doesn't mean you should automatically reject it. If the numbers work, it might still be a good deal.

2. **Risk factors require adjustment, not rejection** - If a property has some risks (like low occupancy), you don't necessarily walk away. Instead, you adjust your return expectations (require higher returns) to compensate for the risk.

3. **Conservative bias protects capital** - When in doubt, be conservative. It's better to miss a good deal than to lose money on a bad one.

4. **If assumptions don't hold, pass or reprice** - If your assumptions about rent growth, expenses, etc. don't make sense when scrutinized, either walk away or negotiate a lower purchase price.

## The 8 Phases: Step-by-Step Walkthrough

Imagine you're considering buying a 180-unit apartment building built in 1990, currently 80% occupied, in a secondary market. Here's what the workflow would do:

---

### PHASE 1: Deal Screening
**What it is:** The initial "quick look" to see if there are any deal-killers.

**What happens:** The system looks for "red flags" - things that would make a deal completely unacceptable. Think of these as automatic disqualifiers.

**Red flags include:**
- Structural problems that can't be fixed
- Environmental contamination (like toxic waste)
- Located in a flood zone with no protection
- High crime area (more than 2.5x the national average)
- Population declining year after year
- Single large employer that could close and destroy the local economy

**Also checked:** Risk factors that aren't deal-killers, but require you to expect higher returns:
- Old building (but still acceptable if numbers work)
- Low occupancy (but you can adjust your expectations)
- Deferred maintenance (but you budget for it)

**Outcome:** Either "this deal has a fatal flaw, stop here" or "no fatal flaws, proceed to detailed analysis (but remember these risk factors)."

**Real example:** A 40-year-old building with 75% occupancy might get flagged for "old age + low occupancy" but NOT rejected. Instead, you'd expect 1.5% higher returns to compensate for the risk.

---

### PHASE 2: Market Analysis
**What it is:** Understanding the health of the local market where the property is located.

**What happens:** The system scores the market (0-100) based on:
- Population growth trends
- Job growth and major employers
- Income levels
- Rent growth history
- Competition (how many similar properties exist)

**Why it matters:** A property in a strong market is less risky than one in a weak market. A weak market might mean you can't raise rents as much as you hoped, or it might be harder to sell later.

**Outcome:** A market score. High scores (70+) are good. Lower scores mean you should expect higher returns or be more cautious.

**Real example:** A property in a growing city with major employers, population growth, and healthy rent growth might score 75/100. A property in a declining industrial town might score 45/100.

---

### PHASE 3: Revenue Underwriting (Rent Validation)
**What it is:** Checking if the seller's projections about future rental income make sense.

**What happens:** The system validates:
- Current rents (what tenants are actually paying now)
- Market rents (what similar apartments in the area rent for)
- Projected rents (what the seller thinks you can get after renovations)
- The "rent premium" (how much more you're trying to charge vs. current rent)

**Why it matters:** Sellers often make aggressive assumptions about how much rent they can charge after renovations. This phase checks if those assumptions are realistic.

**Outcome:** Either "rent assumptions are reasonable" or "rent assumptions are too aggressive - adjust expectations."

**Real example:** If current rent is $1,000/month and the seller projects $1,300/month after renovation, that's a 30% increase. The system checks if this is realistic for a 35-year-old building (might cap it at 15-20% for old buildings).

---

### PHASE 4: Operating Expenses (Tax, Insurance, etc.)
**What it is:** Figuring out what it costs to run the property each year (beyond the mortgage).

**What happens:** The system calculates:
- Property taxes (and this is tricky - taxes don't always reassess at 100% of purchase price. In Florida, it might be 70%. In Georgia, 40%. This matters a LOT.)
- Insurance
- Property management fees
- Maintenance and repairs
- Utilities (if paid by owner)
- Other operational costs

**Why it matters:** Expenses directly reduce your profit. If you underestimate expenses, you'll make less money than projected.

**Real example:** You buy a property for $10 million. You might assume taxes are based on $10 million, but in Florida, they reassess at 70% = $7 million. This saves you thousands per year vs. what you might have estimated.

**Outcome:** A detailed expense projection for Year 1 and future years.

---

### PHASE 5: Capital Expenditure Planning (Renovation Budget)
**What it is:** Planning and validating the renovation budget and its return on investment.

**What happens:** The system evaluates:
- Total renovation cost (how much you'll spend fixing up units)
- Cost per unit (is $12,000/unit reasonable?)
- Return on investment (if you spend $2 million on renovations, will the rent increases justify it?)
- Timeline (how long will renovations take?)

**Why it matters:** Renovations are expensive. You need to make sure the extra rent you'll collect justifies the money you spend.

**Outcome:** Either "renovation budget is reasonable and ROI is good" or "renovation ROI is too low - adjust budget or expectations."

**Real example:** You plan to spend $12,000 per unit renovating 180 units = $2.16 million. If this only increases rent by $50/month, that's a poor ROI. The system might flag this and require 8%+ ROI minimum.

---

### PHASE 6: Financing Structure (The Loan)
**What it is:** Figuring out how much you'll borrow and whether the property can afford the loan payments.

**What happens:** The system calculates:
- Loan amount (typically 65% of purchase price)
- Interest rate
- Monthly/annual loan payments
- Debt Service Coverage Ratio (DSCR) - this is critical: it measures if the property's income can cover the loan payments
- Total equity needed (your down payment + closing costs + renovation budget)

**Why it matters:** If the property can't generate enough income to cover loan payments, you'll have to pay out of pocket every month (bad!). DSCR should be at least 1.25x (meaning income is 25% higher than loan payments).

**Outcome:** Either "financing works - property can cover loan payments comfortably" or "DSCR is too low - financing is risky."

**Real example:** Property generates $1 million/year in net income. Loan payment is $750,000/year. DSCR = $1M / $750K = 1.33x. That's good! But if loan payment was $900K/year, DSCR = 1.11x. That's risky and might be flagged.

---

### PHASE 7: Returns Analysis (Will I Make Money?)
**What it is:** Calculating the actual returns you'll get from this investment and comparing them to your required "hurdles" (minimum acceptable returns).

**What happens:** The system calculates several return metrics:
- **IRR (Internal Rate of Return)** - The annualized return percentage over the life of the investment
- **Cash-on-Cash (CoC)** - Annual cash return divided by your equity investment
- **Equity Multiple** - How many times you get your money back (1.5x means you get back 1.5x your investment)

Then it compares these to your "hurdles" (minimum acceptable returns), which were adjusted in Phase 1 based on risk factors.

**Why it matters:** This is the bottom line. Are the returns high enough to justify the risk? The system might require 17.5% IRR for a secondary market property with moderate risks, but 19% IRR if the property is older and has lower occupancy.

**Outcome:** Either "returns meet hurdles - looks good" or "returns below hurdles - either renegotiate price or walk away."

**Real example:** 
- Your adjusted hurdle is 17.5% IRR (because of old building + low occupancy)
- Projected IRR is 18.2%
- This passes! ✅
- But if projected IRR was only 16.5%, it fails ❌

---

### PHASE 8: Risk Assessment & Final Recommendation
**What it is:** Putting everything together and making the final decision: Proceed, Proceed with Caution, Request Repricing, or Pass (walk away).

**What happens:** The system:
- Reviews all findings from previous phases
- Counts how many phases passed vs. failed
- Counts warnings and concerns
- Makes a final recommendation

**Recommendation Types:**
1. **PROCEED** - Everything looks good. All phases passed, no major warnings. Green light to move forward.
2. **PROCEED WITH CAUTION** - Deal works, but there are some concerns (1-2 warnings). Proceed, but be careful and monitor those risks.
3. **REQUEST REPRICING** - Deal almost works, but not quite. Multiple concerns. Try to negotiate a lower purchase price to make the numbers work.
4. **PASS** - Deal doesn't work. Either fatal flaws, returns too low, or too many risks. Walk away.

**Outcome:** A clear recommendation with reasoning.

**Real example:**
- All 8 phases passed
- Returns meet hurdles
- 1 warning: "Rent assumptions slightly aggressive"
- **Recommendation: PROCEED WITH CAUTION**
- Reasoning: "Deal workable but requires attention to flagged items"

---

## How It All Fits Together: The Complete Flow

Here's how the phases connect:

```
1. SCREENING → "No fatal flaws? Continue. (Adjust return expectations for risks.)"
   ↓
2. MARKET ANALYSIS → "Is the market healthy? (Score the market strength.)"
   ↓
3. REVENUE → "Are rent projections realistic? (Validate assumptions.)"
   ↓
4. EXPENSES → "What will it cost to operate? (Calculate all expenses.)"
   ↓
5. CAPEX → "Is renovation budget reasonable? (Check ROI.)"
   ↓
6. FINANCING → "Can the property afford the loan? (Calculate DSCR.)"
   ↓
7. RETURNS → "Will I make enough money? (Calculate IRR, CoC, compare to hurdles.)"
   ↓
8. FINAL DECISION → "Put it all together. What's the recommendation?"
```

**Important:** If Phase 1 (Screening) finds a red flag, the workflow stops early. No point analyzing financing if the building has toxic waste!

## Key Concepts Explained Simply

### IRR (Internal Rate of Return)
**Simple explanation:** If you invest $1 million today and get back $1.75 million in 5 years, your IRR is roughly 15%. It's the annualized return percentage.

**Why it matters:** It's the standard metric for comparing investments. Higher is better. You need to beat your "hurdle" (minimum acceptable return).

### DSCR (Debt Service Coverage Ratio)
**Simple explanation:** If the property makes $1 million per year and loan payments are $800,000 per year, DSCR = 1.25x. The "1.25x" means income is 25% higher than debt payments.

**Why it matters:** Lenders require minimum DSCR (usually 1.25x). Below this, they won't lend. Above this, you have a safety cushion.

### Cash-on-Cash (CoC)
**Simple explanation:** If you invest $1 million and get $70,000 in cash each year, your CoC is 7% ($70K / $1M).

**Why it matters:** It shows your annual cash return. Higher is better. Different property ages require different minimums (6-8% typically).

### Hurdles (Minimum Acceptable Returns)
**Simple explanation:** Your minimum acceptable return based on risk. A risky deal needs higher returns. A safe deal can accept lower returns.

**Why it matters:** If a deal's projected returns don't beat your hurdle, you shouldn't invest. You're not getting paid enough for the risk you're taking.

**Example:** 
- Base hurdle for secondary market: 17.5% IRR
- Old building (+100 basis points): 18.5% IRR
- Low occupancy (+150 basis points): 20.0% IRR
- Your deal must achieve at least 20.0% IRR to proceed

### Basis Points (bps)
**Simple explanation:** 1 basis point = 0.01%. 100 basis points = 1%. Used to talk about small percentage adjustments.

**Example:** "Add 150 bps to the hurdle" means "add 1.5% to the required return."

## A Complete Example (Simplified)

Let's say you're evaluating a 180-unit apartment building:

**Phase 1 - Screening:**
- 35 years old (risk factor, not fatal)
- 78% occupancy (risk factor, not fatal)
- No red flags
- **Result:** Proceed, but expect +250 bps (2.5%) higher returns

**Phase 2 - Market:**
- Growing secondary market
- **Score:** 72/100
- **Result:** Market is healthy, proceed

**Phase 3 - Revenue:**
- Current rent: $1,000/unit
- Projected rent: $1,180/unit (18% increase)
- **Result:** Reasonable for property age, proceed

**Phase 4 - Expenses:**
- Taxes: $129,000/year (70% reassessment)
- Total expenses: $850,000/year
- **Result:** Expenses calculated, proceed

**Phase 5 - Capex:**
- Renovation: $2.16M ($12K/unit)
- ROI: 9.2%
- **Result:** Good ROI, proceed

**Phase 6 - Financing:**
- Loan: $8.25M (65% of $12.7M purchase)
- Annual debt: $475,000
- Net income: $650,000
- DSCR: 1.37x
- **Result:** Strong DSCR, proceed

**Phase 7 - Returns:**
- Projected IRR: 18.7%
- Required hurdle: 17.5% + 2.5% (from Phase 1) = 20.0%
- **Result:** ❌ IRR 18.7% < Hurdle 20.0% = FAIL

**Phase 8 - Final Decision:**
- 7 phases passed
- 1 phase failed (returns)
- Multiple warnings (old building, low occupancy, returns below hurdle)
- **Recommendation: REQUEST REPRICING**
- **Reasoning:** "Deal almost works but returns don't meet risk-adjusted hurdles. Negotiate purchase price down to make returns meet 20% hurdle."

## Summary: What This Workflow Does

The Shieldstone master workflow is a **systematic, defensible process** for evaluating apartment building investments. It:

1. **Screens for deal-killers** early (saves time)
2. **Adjusts return expectations** based on risks (rather than auto-rejecting)
3. **Validates all assumptions** (rent, expenses, renovations, financing)
4. **Calculates returns** and compares them to risk-adjusted hurdles
5. **Makes a clear recommendation** with reasoning

The goal is to make **informed, data-driven investment decisions** rather than relying on gut feelings or arbitrary rules.

---

## Why This System Exists

Real estate investors need a consistent, repeatable process to evaluate deals. Without a system like this, you might:
- Miss important risks
- Make decisions based on emotions
- Inconsistently evaluate similar deals
- Lack defensible reasoning for your decisions

This workflow ensures every deal gets the same thorough analysis, and every recommendation has clear reasoning backed by data.
